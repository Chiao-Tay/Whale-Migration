Chapter 1

Meet the travellers

Which whales visit Australia?

Many kinds of whale pass our coast.

But when people spot a whale, it's almost always one of two: the humpback or the southern right whale.

Each sighting is one record of a whale that someone saw and logged. We use 14,019 sightings from 2016 to 2025.

Below, we'll see who visits most, when each one comes, and which states see the most kinds.

Whales visit every state, but they don't wander at random. They follow a route.

Chapter 2

The whale highway

Where do whales go?

Whales return to the same stretches of sea year after year.

They use them to travel, feed, rest and have calves.

The government has marked 51 of these important areas. Put them together and they form a highway around almost the whole coast.

The first map shows where the highway is. The second uses real sightings to show when it's busy.

51

important areas form a coastal highway that fills in winter and empties in summer.

Now watch it through the year: almost empty in summer, full by July–August.

But the highway doesn't fill up all at once. The whales arrive in a wave.

Chapter 3

The migration wave

When can I see whales near me?

In early winter, humpbacks swim north to warm water to have their calves.

In spring, they swim back south to feed in Antarctica.

So each part of the coast has its own whale season.

The first chart shows the whole coast, from north to south. The second zooms in on seven east-coast towns.

2 waves

pass Sydney each year: north in June, south in October. The best month depends on where you stand.

Zoom in on seven towns: Sydney sees whales twice, in June and October.

Today humpbacks pass us in their thousands. Sixty years ago, they almost disappeared.

Chapter 4

Hunted to the brink

How close did we come to losing them?

From the 1920s to the 1960s, whaling stations on both coasts hunted humpbacks.

Their records list 23,057 whales.

Hunting was banned in 1963, but only when almost none were left.

In the chart below, look for three moments: a long pause, a peak in the 1950s, and a sudden collapse in 1962–63.

3.5–5%

of humpbacks were left when hunting was finally banned in 1963.

Since the ban, humpbacks have slowly come back. Today we watch them with cameras instead of harpoons.

Chapter 5

Eyes on the water

Who counts the whales today?

Most whale sightings aren't recorded by scientists.

They're recorded by people like you, on their phones.

Since 2016, 56% of sightings came through free apps and websites like iNaturalist and Happywhale.

The first chart shows who logs whale sightings today. The second shows how that changed since 2000. Green means apps and websites in both.

56%

of whale sightings now come from ordinary people using free apps.

It wasn't always this way. In 2000, apps and websites sent in just 3 sightings.

More sightings don't always mean more whales.

Sometimes it simply means more people are looking, like the boom in app sightings above.

That's why many charts in this story use percentages instead of totals.

You could be one of those people. Here's where and when to look.

Chapter 6

Your turn

Where and when should I go?

A few spots stand out.

South coast: Head of Bight (SA) and Warrnambool (VIC) for southern right whales.

East and west coasts: Hervey Bay, Byron Bay and Augusta for humpbacks.
