A data story about Australia's whales

The Humpback Highway

Every winter, whales travel thousands of kilometres along our coastline. Where do they go, when can you see them, and how did they come back from the brink?

Chapter 1

Meet the travellers

Between 2016 and 2025, Australians logged 14,019 reports of baleen whales, the giant filter-feeders that eat krill and small fish. Ten species were reported, but two dominate: humpback whales and southern right whales make up nearly 98% of all reports.

Each species keeps its own calendar. Humpbacks are reported mostly from June to October. Southern right whales are concentrated in July and August, when 70% of their reports occur. The few blue whale reports cluster in May and June.

Chapter 2

The great whale highway

Whales don't use the ocean evenly. The Australian Government has mapped 51 Biologically Important Areas for humpback, southern right, blue and sperm whales: stretches of sea where they migrate, feed, rest or raise their calves. Together they trace a highway hugging almost the entire coastline.

The reports show the highway filling and emptying through the year. In January and February, there were just 29 reports around the whole country; the whales are away feeding in Antarctic waters. By July and August, the count climbs to 6,770.

Chapter 3

Following the migration wave

Humpbacks swim north in early winter to give birth and breed in warm water, then head back south in spring. That means the best month to see them depends on where you stand.

Zoom in on the east coast and two waves appear. Sydney has two peaks: whales heading north in June and July, and heading south in September and October. Further south at Eden, 52% of reports come in October, as mothers and calves pass on the way back. Further north, Hervey Bay peaks in August, when whales rest in its sheltered waters.

Chapter 4

Hunted to the brink

The whales we watch today are survivors. Whaling stations once operated on both coasts, and the records hold 23,057 humpbacks caught between 1925 and 1963.

~2,000humpbacks caught each year, 1953–1957
91caught in 1963, the last year of humpback catches in the records

Catches fell from 1,483 in 1961 to 728 in 1962 and just 91 in 1963. After hunting stopped, humpbacks slowly recovered, and today they are once again a common winter sight.

Chapter 5

Eyes on the water

Most of what we know about where whales go today comes from ordinary people. Since 2016, 56% of whale reports came through citizen-science apps such as iNaturalist and Happywhale, where anyone can upload a photo of a whale.

The boom is recent. In 2000, just 3 reports came through citizen-science apps. In 2023 there were 1,876. (The tall bump around 2008–2012 is mostly one large scientific survey in the Kimberley.)

This is also a reminder about reading these charts. More reports can mean more whales, but it can also mean more people looking. That's why the earlier charts compare shares of reports rather than raw totals.

Chapter 6

Your turn: witness the journey

Whales are reported all around the coast, but some spots stand out. The Head of Bight in South Australia is the hotspot for southern right whales, while Hervey Bay, Byron Bay and Augusta are humpback favourites.
